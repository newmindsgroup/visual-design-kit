# Naming

Version-Timestamp: 2026-10-20 02:07:10 UTC

A good name is distinctive in its category, easy to say and spell in every target market, legally available enough to be worth a lawyer's time, and gives the designer something to draw. Generate widely, screen hard, and present few names in context.

## Naming brief

Freeze these before generating:

- **What is named:** company, product, feature, service, sub-brand, campaign, place. Its role in the architecture (masterbrand, endorsed, standalone). See [identity system](identity-system.md#brand-architecture-models).
- **Positioning and attributes** from the platform; the 2-3 ideas the name could carry.
- **Markets and languages,** including spoken languages of the sales team and major customer groups.
- **Constraints:** length, must-contain or must-avoid sounds, existing family naming patterns, domain expectations (exact .com or not), app store presence.
- **Criteria with weights,** agreed before candidates exist (see the evaluation table).

## Typologies

| Type | Examples (public brands) | Strengths | Risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive | General Motors, The Weather Channel | Instantly clear, low media cost | Weak trademark, hard to stand out, limits expansion |
| Suggestive | Netflix, Pinterest, Mailchimp | Hints at benefit, protectable | Can drift toward descriptive in crowded fields |
| Compound or blend | Microsoft, Instagram, YouTube | Clear and ownable | Blends can read as forced or clumsy |
| Real word, arbitrary | Apple, Amazon, Slack | Memorable, strong trademark in an unrelated class | Needs media to attach meaning; .com rarely free |
| Metaphor or myth | Nike, Oracle, Patagonia | Rich story, visual potential | Cultural meanings vary by market |
| Coined or invented | Kodak, Xerox, Häagen-Dazs | Strongest trademark, free domains | No built-in meaning; spelling and pronunciation must be taught |
| Modified spelling | Lyft, Flickr, Tumblr | Real-word sound, available domain | Spelling errors, looks dated when the trend passes |
| Founder or eponymous | Ford, Dior, Ben & Jerry's | Heritage, personality | Tied to a person's reputation; hard to sell the business |
| Acronym or initialism | IBM, BMW, H&M | Short | Meaningless until famous; usually earned, not chosen |
| Classical or foreign roots | Volvo ("I roll"), Audi ("listen") | Elegant, layered meaning | Mispronunciation, unintended meanings elsewhere |
| Playful or sound-led | Coca-Cola, PayPal, Hubba Bubba | Rhyme and alliteration aid memory | Can feel childish in serious categories |

Legal strength generally rises from generic (unprotectable) to descriptive (weak), suggestive, arbitrary and fanciful or coined (strongest). This is the spectrum US courts use (from *Abercrombie & Fitch v. Hunting World*, 1976); most other systems reward distinctiveness in a similar way.

## Generation techniques

Aim for 200-400 raw candidates across 4-6 territories. Volume is cheap; screening is where quality comes from.

1. **Territory mapping.** From the platform, write 4-6 territories (for example "motion and flow", "craft and making", "light", "place of origin", "the customer's outcome"). Generate 40-80 candidates per territory.
2. **Word banks.** For each territory list roots, synonyms, metaphors, objects, verbs, places, myths and technical terms in English and the key market languages. Use etymology dictionaries for Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Old Norse or other roots relevant to the story.
3. **Morphing.** Truncate (Fedex from Federal Express), blend two roots, add or swap a prefix or suffix (-ly, -io, -era, -ia, -o), change one letter, double a letter, reverse syllables. Watch for overused suffix trends that date a name quickly.
4. **Compounding.** Pair an unexpected noun with a benefit word, or two concrete nouns ("Mail" + "chimp").
5. **Sound symbolism.** Front vowels (i, e) and voiceless stops (k, t, p) read small, quick and sharp; back vowels (o, u) and sonorants (m, l, b) read large, round and soft (the bouba/kiki effect). Match sound to the attributes: a fast fintech app and a slow-living furniture brand want different phonetics.
6. **Constraint passes.** Two syllables only; must start with a hard consonant; must work as a verb; must sound good after "Hey".
7. **Using Claude for volume.** Generate per territory with a one-line rationale per candidate, ask for 50 at a time, then filter with the criteria. Never treat model output as evidence that a name is unused, pronounceable abroad or legally available.

Cut 400 to about 30 on criteria alone, screen 30 to about 10 with the checks below, present 3-5.

## Evaluation criteria

| Criterion | Test | Typical weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distinctiveness | Differs from the 15 nearest competitor names in sound, look and meaning | 20% |
| Fit | Supports the positioning or at least does not contradict it | 15% |
| Say-it, spell-it | Say it once to 5 people; ask them to type it. Count errors | 15% |
| Length | 2-3 syllables, 4-10 letters as a common starting point | 10% |
| Memorability | Recall after 24 hours from a list of 10 | 10% |
| Extendability | Survives new products, markets and acquisitions | 10% |
| Visual potential | Interesting letterforms, symmetry, a strong initial for a monogram | 5% |
| Linguistic risk | Passes native-speaker screening in every target market | Pass/fail |
| Legal risk | Passes the knockout search below | Pass/fail |
| Digital availability | Acceptable domain and handles exist | 15% |

## Linguistic screening

Do this per target market with native speakers, not machine translation alone.

- **Meaning:** literal translation, slang, vulgar or religious meanings, regional variants (Spanish in Mexico vs Spain vs Argentina; Portuguese in Brazil vs Portugal).
- **Sound-alikes:** words the name sounds like when spoken with the local accent.
- **Pronunciation:** sounds that do not exist or merge in the language ("th" for many speakers; r/l in Japanese and Korean; v/b in Spanish; final consonant clusters in Mandarin speakers' English).
- **Script:** how it transliterates into Cyrillic, Arabic, Hebrew, Devanagari, Thai, Japanese katakana and Korean Hangul; whether the transliteration has meanings of its own.
- **Chinese markets:** decide early between phonetic, semantic or phono-semantic Chinese names. Coca-Cola's 可口可乐 ("tasty and joyful") is the classic phono-semantic example. Count strokes and check character meanings with native speakers and a local naming specialist.
- **Cautionary precedents:** Mitsubishi sold the Pajero as Montero in Spanish-speaking markets because "pajero" is vulgar slang; Honda's Fit was renamed Jazz in some markets after the original name "Fitta" proved vulgar in Nordic languages. The Chevrolet Nova "no va" story is widely repeated but largely a myth; do not cite it.
- **Record** each check: market, reviewer, date, findings, verdict.

## Domains, handles and digital presence

- **Domains:** check the exact .com first, then acceptable patterns: get-, try-, use-, -hq, -app, a country ccTLD for local businesses, or category TLDs (.ai, .io, .studio). Price matters: premium aftermarket domains can cost more than the identity. Check ccTLD registration rules before relying on one.
- **Handles:** Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, X, LinkedIn, Threads, Facebook, Pinterest, plus GitHub and npm for developer tools. Consistent handles across platforms beat the perfect name on one.
- **App stores:** search the Apple App Store and Google Play for identical or confusable names in the same category.
- **Search:** search the name in quotes, with the category word, and in image search. A dominant meaning (a disease, a celebrity, a scandal) is a warning.
- **Company registries:** Companies House (UK), state business registries (US), and the equivalent in each market.

Availability of a domain or handle says nothing about trademark rights.

## Trademark knockout search

This is a preliminary screen to drop obviously conflicting candidates before paying for legal work. **It is not legal clearance.** Only a qualified trademark attorney's full clearance search and opinion can support adoption, and the client decides with that advice.

Starting points (free, official):

| Database | Coverage | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [USPTO Trademark Search](https://tmsearch.uspto.gov/) | US federal applications and registrations | Replaced TESS on 30 November 2023; does not cover state or unregistered common-law marks |
| [WIPO Global Brand Database](https://branddb.wipo.int/) | International (Madrid) registrations plus many national collections | Good first multi-country pass; supports image search |
| [EUIPO eSearch plus](https://euipo.europa.eu/eSearch/) | EU trade marks | Use [TMview](https://www.tmdn.org/tmview/) to search many national EU offices at once |
| [UK IPO trade mark search](https://www.gov.uk/search-for-trademark) | UK registrations | Separate from the EU since 2021 |
| National offices | For example CIPO (Canada), IP Australia, J-PlatPat (Japan), CNIPA (China), KIPRIS (Korea), INPI (Brazil) | Search every market on the launch roadmap |

Method:

1. Identify the relevant Nice classes (the international classification has 45: 1-34 goods, 35-45 services). Software is commonly class 9 and 42, retail 35, finance 36, food 29-30 and 43. Confirm the classes with counsel.
2. Search the exact name, then phonetic and visual equivalents (Kwik for Quick, swapped vowels, plural, spaced and joined forms) in the relevant and adjacent classes.
3. Search unregistered use with web, app store and social searches; in the US, prior use can create rights without registration.
4. Note first-to-file jurisdictions (China and much of the world): plan early filings there with counsel.
5. Record for each candidate: database, date, query, classes, hits and a red/amber/green note. Hand this log to the attorney.

## Presenting names

- Present 3-5 finalists, never a list of 50.
- Show each name in context: set in a neutral typeface at headline size, in a sentence, as a URL and handle, spoken aloud (record audio), on a mock app icon or storefront, and in the key non-Latin scripts.
- Give the story in two sentences, the scores against the criteria, and the known risks (linguistic, legal, domain).
- State status explicitly: proposed, shortlisted, cleared for design exploration, legal clearance pending, approved.
- Pass exact spelling, capitalization, diacritics and permitted abbreviations to logo work only after approval.

## Common failures

- One territory explored 400 ways → enforce 4-6 territories before volume.
- Names chosen because the .com was free → domain is one weighted criterion, not the filter.
- Trendy suffixes (-ify, -ly, -io) that date the name → use them only when the sound genuinely fits the attributes.
- Machine translation as the only linguistic check → native speakers in each market, recorded.
- Saying "this name is available" → say "no conflicting marks found in [database] for [classes] on [date]; legal clearance required".
