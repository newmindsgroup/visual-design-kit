# Sage

## Essence

- **Core desire:** to find the truth and use intelligence to understand the world.
- **Fear:** being duped, misled or ignorant.
- **Promise:** "We will help you understand, so you can decide well." Clarity and evidence over persuasion.
- **Mark and Pearson group:** independence and fulfillment (with Innocent and Explorer). Strategy: seek information and analysis, then share it.
- **Shadow:** aloofness, jargon, endless analysis with no recommendation.

Archetypes are a positioning shorthand popularized by Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson (2001), drawn from Jung; they are not a scientific taxonomy. Strong brands usually blend a primary and a secondary archetype, so decide which one sets the visual system and which one flavors it.

## Voice and tone

- Precise, calm, evidence-first. Define terms, show sources, state uncertainty ("about 40%, based on 1,200 responses").
- Lead with the finding, then the method. Sentences of 15-22 words; active voice; no hype adjectives.
- Invite the reader to check the work: footnotes, methodology links, dates on every claim.
- Headline register: "What the data says about remote onboarding." Avoid questions that tease and answers that hide.

## Fits and tensions

- **Fits:** education, research and publishing, consulting, legal and professional services, B2B software that analyzes, health information, think tanks, museums, libraries, data products.
- **Common blends:** Sage + Ruler (consulting, institutional finance); Sage + Creator (design-led research, technical publishing); Sage + Caregiver (medical information); Sage + Explorer (science communication).
- **Tensions:** pure Sage can feel cold, slow and exclusive; add one human device (a real author photo, a warm accent, plain-language summaries). Against Jester or Outlaw it reads as a contradiction unless used for irony.
- **Channel notes:** thrives in long-form web, reports, decks (`channel.presentation`) and email newsletters; weaker in impulse retail or out-of-home where there is no room to explain.

## Clichés to avoid

- Owls, open books, lightbulbs, graduation caps, brain icons, glowing network meshes: replace with real artifacts from the field (a lab notebook page, a ledger, a map).
- Navy-and-gold "heritage" palette with Times New Roman: pick a contemporary text serif and a distinct accent.
- Dense gray walls of text with no hierarchy: authority comes from structure, not volume.
- AI-generated "wise old man" or floating-book imagery.

## Reference anchors

- Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson, *The Hero and the Outlaw* (2001); Carl G. Jung, *The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious*
- Google (search and Scholar), TED, BBC, Harvard Business Review, The Economist: commonly cited Sage brands
- Robert Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* (for reading typography)
- Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (for evidence-led graphics)
- Public design systems with strong reading typography: GOV.UK Design System, IBM Carbon
