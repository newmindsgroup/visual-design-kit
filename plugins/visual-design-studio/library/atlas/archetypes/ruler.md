# Ruler

## Essence

- **Core desire:** control; creating order and prosperity out of chaos.
- **Fear:** chaos, being overthrown.
- **Promise:** "We set the standard; with us, things are in order." Authority, assurance and status.
- **Mark and Pearson group:** stability and control (with Caregiver and Creator). Strategy: exercise power, set the rules.
- **Shadow:** arrogance, rigidity, exclusion, "because we say so".

Archetypes are a positioning shorthand (Mark and Pearson, 2001; after Jung), not science. Ruler is often a position of incumbency, which invites challengers; the modern version is leadership by service ("we take responsibility"), not dominance.

## Voice and tone

- Measured, formal but not stiff; complete sentences; the third person for institutions, second person for service.
- Quantify authority: years, assets, jurisdictions, certifications, track record.
- No slang, no exclamation marks; humor, if any, is dry understatement.
- Headline register: "Since 1952, we have kept our word." Avoid arrogance: "The best" claims need evidence.

## Fits and tensions

- **Fits:** banking, wealth management, law, accounting, government institutions, premium automotive, watchmaking, hospitality, insurance, universities, large B2B enterprises, high-end real estate.
- **Common blends:** Ruler + Sage (consulting, institutional research); Ruler + Caregiver (insurers, public services); Ruler + Lover (luxury houses); Ruler + Creator (premium design-led brands); Ruler + Hero (defense and aerospace). Name the primary archetype that governs type and palette and the secondary that adds warmth or edge in one place.
- **Tensions:** Ruler vs approachability: incumbents with a Ruler look can feel cold to younger audiences (`audience.teens-gen-z`); add warmth through photography, plain language and one humane detail. Ruler vs challenger spaces such as `industry.crypto-web3` where trust is earned by openness, not formality.
- **Price tier:** Ruler scales from `tier.premium` to `tier.ultra-luxury`; at a value tier it reads as pretension unless the institution itself is the product (a bank, a utility).

## Clichés to avoid

- Navy and gold with a crest or lion on every institution; shields, columns and globes in logos: choose one precise detail (a monogram, a rule system, a numeral style).
- Times New Roman and drop shadows; pseudo-premium bevels.
- Stock handshakes, men in suits staring at skylines, glass-tower imagery.
- Ornamental excess: Ruler is about restraint and order, not decoration.

## Reference anchors

- Margaret Mark and Carol S. Pearson, *The Hero and the Outlaw* (2001); Carl G. Jung's archetype theory
- Rolex, Mercedes-Benz, American Express, Rolls-Royce, Microsoft (corporate positioning): commonly cited Ruler brands
- Massimo Vignelli's American Airlines and Knoll identities for Ruler expressed through modernist order
- Eric Gill's lettering and Edward Johnston's London Underground typeface for institutional typography
- Public-sector heraldry and identity manuals (for example NASA Graphics Standards Manual, 1975) as examples of disciplined systems
