07 Imagery & iconsالصور والأيقونات

Fabric and light first. Faces are optional and always the model’s choice.

Photograph garments in warm late-afternoon light through arches and screens, on models or on forms, with the fabric moving. Crops favor drape, hand embroidery and texture; when a model appears, styling and coverage follow the collection, and faces are shown only with consent and by preference. No desert-and-camel clichés, no gold-on-black arabesque wallpaper.

Icons are 1.25px hairlines with round ends on a 24px grid. Directional icons (arrows, the delivery van, progress) are mirrored for RTL; symmetrical ones (heart, bag) are not.

10 Rationaleمبررات التصميم

Why light and arches, and not gold arabesque.

Why it fits

Gulf luxury customers shop in Arabic on their phones and expect the experience to be native, not translated. Building right-to-left first (logical CSS properties, mirrored layout and icons, Naskh typography with real Arabic line-height) is itself the luxury signal.

“Noor” means light. The pointed arch and the lattice borrow from Gulf architecture with restraint; the fabric, lit from above, carries the emotion.

What it risks

  • Amiri is classical; younger clients may read it as formal. Test against a modern Naskh or Kufi display (Reem Kufi, Noto Kufi Arabic) with customers.
  • Numerals, gendered address and modesty in imagery are client and audience decisions; validate with native-speaking copywriters and the client.
  • Fonts are subset to this page’s text. Production needs full Arabic coverage (and Persian/Urdu if those markets matter).
  • Confirm UAE e-commerce, VAT display and consumer-protection rules with counsel.

The expected alternative

Black and gold, an arabesque border on everything, a calligraphic logo in Thuluth and a falcon or a dune in the hero. It signals “Gulf luxury” generically and fights the garments for attention. The adjacent option is a Western minimal luxury site translated into Arabic; it is clean but feels imported.
