Spot the default. Fix it on purpose.

Eighteen patterns that make a design look machine-made, each rendered twice at real size: the first answer a model reaches for, and a better version built for a specific brief. The better versions span eighteen styles and industries on purpose.

A pattern is a tell when it appears by default. A purple gradient, a glass panel or a centered hero is fine when the brief asks for it and the execution earns it.

01

Default indigo-to-pink gradient

AT-01 / default-gradient

What changed: the palette now comes from the domain (asphalt, hi-vis orange, lane-marking white) instead of the default wash. The energy the gradient was faking moves into a live route strip that shows the product and a hazard-chevron edge. The copy names who it is for and what they see.

02

One sans for everything

AT-02 / one-sans-everywhere

What changed: three typographic voices with three jobs: an italic serif masthead with character, a soft serif for dish names, a compact grotesk for labels and descriptions. Prices drop the currency symbol and decimals and line up as tabular figures, the way a confident menu reads.

03

Centered hero and three icon cards

AT-03 / centered-hero-three-cards

What changed: the centered stack and three interchangeable cards become a flush-left working grid: a claim only this studio can make, a section drawing that proves it, and an index of real projects with dates and areas. Red marks the daylight and the project numbers, nothing else.

04

Emoji as icons

AT-04 / emoji-icons

What changed: emoji render differently on every platform and carry a jokey tone into a clinical setting. One drawn icon set (24px grid, 1.75px stroke, round caps, one color) replaces them, the copy loses its exclamation marks and gains facts, and the urgent route gets its own calm panel.

05

Icons in pastel circles

AT-05 / pastel-icon-circles

What changed: the icons were decoration pretending to be information, and the four steps were not steps. For a task people must finish, the page says what it costs, how long it takes and what to have ready, then offers one start button. No icon survives because none was needed.

06

Uniform soft cards everywhere

AT-06 / uniform-soft-cards

What changed: six equal cards told the user that everything matters equally. The balance is now the one large thing, followed by one primary action and a ruled list of what happened today. Hairlines and space do the grouping; there is no shadow on the screen at all.

07

Glassmorphism on everything

AT-07 / gratuitous-glass

What changed: glass survives in one place, the nav bar over the landscape, where it shows depth and carries a 72% tint so white text clears 4.5:1. Dates, guests and the action sit on an opaque bar. The blurred blobs gave the glass something to blur; the place itself is now the picture.

08

Gradient text headline

AT-08 / gradient-text

What changed: a solid color at full strength reads louder than a gradient fill, survives screenshots and forced-colors mode, and has one contrast ratio you can check. The energy now comes from scale, a tight condensed face and a single volt slab that carries the practical details.

09

Glowing orbs and blobs

AT-09 / glow-orbs

What changed: the orbs stood in for “energy” and “AI” without saying anything. The better hero shows the product’s actual insight, a 12-hour carbon-intensity curve with the cleanest window marked in yellow, and turns it into one action. The decoration is the data.

10

Fake dashboard hero

AT-10 / fake-dashboard-hero

What changed: a tilted wall of unreadable charts says “software” and nothing else. The better hero crops one real moment at 1:1 scale, an invoice and its timeline, so a visitor can read the promise in the product itself. It bleeds off the edge to imply the rest of the app without faking it.

11

Sparkles for anything AI

AT-11 / ai-sparkles

What changed: “Magic” hides what will happen. The better panel names the action and its inputs, labels the output as an AI draft that a person checks, lists its sources and offers insert or discard. No sparkle, no purple: the feature earns trust by being specific.

12

Blob-people illustration

AT-12 / blob-people

What changed: interchangeable faceless people became a drawing of the thing members actually do: a bike on a repair stand, tools on the pegboard. Two riso inks (blue line, fluorescent pink fill with halftone), a slight misregistration and condensed caps give a print-shop voice no stock illustration pack has.

13

Meaningless metrics

AT-14 / empty-metrics

What changed: each number now names its unit, population and period, and one of them is unflattering on purpose. Specific, checkable figures read as evidence; “10x” and “99.9%” read as decoration and invite distrust. A source line closes the band.

14

Vague promise copy

AT-15 / vague-promise + AT-24 / copy-tells

What changed: the promise became an outcome a student can picture and check: what they will make, how long it takes, how many people share the room and what it costs. Gone are the em-dash reveals, the “not just X, it’s Y” turn and the anytime-anywhere filler.

15

Low-contrast gray body text

AT-17 / gray-body-text

What changed: body text moves from #A3A3A3 at 14px light (2.5:1) to #22201C at 18px regular (15.6:1) with a 1.6 line-height and a 60-character measure. Muted color is kept for metadata, where #5E5A52 still clears 6.5:1. Same words; now they can be read.

16

Too many calls to action

AT-18 / cta-pileup

What changed: four equal buttons, a banner and a “learn more” asked the visitor to choose before they understood. One primary action and one quiet text link now put the decision in order, and the floor plan answers the questions the extra buttons were deferring. The deco frame suits the 1934 building.

17

Badge and pill overload

AT-19 / pill-overload

What changed: when everything is flagged, nothing is. One status line that actually helps the decision (“Back in stock”), a material line that justifies the price and a single action replace ten badges, a fake discount and a star rating with no source. The product image gets the space back.

18

Dark mode with neon glow

AT-20 / neon-dark-mode

What changed: dark interfaces need elevation from lighter surfaces, not halos. One success green, one warning amber, a calm mono log and 7:1-plus text keep the screen readable at 2 a.m.; the neon version spent its contrast on glow and still hid the one warning that mattered.
